# Classroom delivery guide: Why Information Work Needs Technology

Use this guide with the rendered `slides.qmd` deck, `worksheet.md`, and `practical.md`. The slides provide shared prompts; the learning comes from prediction, measurement, classification, explanation, and defended decisions. Do not read this script aloud word for word.

## Timing, preparation, and participation

The lesson is allocated six periods. Timings assume **40–45 minutes per period** and include percentages so they can be scaled. If a period is shortened, preserve the opening retrieval, one substantial learner task, and the individual outcome check; shorten reporting before shortening thinking time.

Prepare the fictional request-code list from `practical.md`, a visible timer, paper, calculators, and—where available—a spreadsheet. For Period 4, prepare example cards from the classifications in the guide. For Period 5, prepare the fictional field-survey scenario. Do not use real service tickets, usernames, learner records, survey responses, patient information, or other identifiable data.

Throughout the sequence:

- Ask a question, allow silent thinking, then invite pair discussion before whole-class answers.
- Require evidence for claims about speed, accuracy, and suitability; “digital is better” is not sufficient.
- After one response, invite another learner to agree, challenge, or improve it.
- Alternate short teacher explanations with learner decisions or demonstrations every three to five minutes.
- Use the frame `requirement → evidence → method → control` when learners recommend technology.

## Period 1 — Experience a manual information workflow (LO-1)

**Outcome check:** Learners use observed workflow evidence to explain why increasing manual work can become slow, inconsistent, or inaccurate.

### 1. Prediction hook — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Display the fictional codes from `practical.md` without totals. Ask: “Which code appears most often? Decide without counting, and rate your confidence from one to five.”

Give **8–10 seconds silent wait time**, then ask learners to compare predictions. Do not confirm the answer.

Say: “A confident impression is not yet dependable evidence. We will perform a manual workflow and measure what happens.”

### 2. Manual tally round — 30% (12–14 minutes)

Learners work individually so discrepancies remain visible. Ask them to copy or tally P, N, and L and record their elapsed time. Start all learners together if possible; learners stop their own timer or note when the visible timer reaches their finish point.

Suggested phrasing: “Accuracy matters more than racing. Preserve the source list, show your tally method, and do not correct a neighbour yet.”

After counting, learners check that category totals sum to 20. Give **20 seconds** to inspect before displaying the expected totals only after everyone has committed: P = 7, N = 8, L = 5.

Ask:

- “Did everyone take the same time?” (**3–5 seconds wait**)
- “Did everyone obtain the same result?” (**5 seconds**)
- “Which part of the workflow created the greatest chance of error?” (**8–10 seconds**)

Record anonymous ranges and discrepancy counts, not names or individual performance.

### 3. Diagnose the workflow — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Pairs annotate the manual process using: repetition, transcription, checking, consistency, storage, retrieval, and dissemination.

Say: “Do not label manual work ‘bad.’ Identify where this method works and where increasing volume changes its fitness for purpose.”

Likely learner responses include losing position in the list, missing or double-counting codes, slow rechecking, and differences between counters. If learners say “people make mistakes,” probe: “Which specific action creates the opportunity, and what procedure could reduce it?”

Misconception response—“manual always means inaccurate”: point to any verified correct tally. Ask: “Was this accurate? What happens to time and checking effort as volume grows?”

### 4. Scale thought experiment — 20% (8–9 minutes)

Ask learners to imagine 20, 200, and 20,000 codes arriving at three locations. In pairs they predict what changes in elapsed time, backlog risk, reconciliation, and retrieval.

Use **10 seconds wait time** before asking for the 20,000-record response. Invite a second learner to identify an assumption in the first answer.

Energy change: conduct a quick continuum—learners point left for manual, right for digital, and centre for hybrid—first for three records, then 20,000. Hear one justification from each side.

### 5. Exit evidence statement — 10% (4–5 minutes)

Prompt: “Bulk manual handling can become ___ because our tally showed ___.” Require a mechanism plus evidence, not a general opinion.

Transition: “We have identified problems under scale. Next we compare methods carefully, including the new risks technology introduces.”

**Differentiation and fallbacks:** Read the code sequence in groups of five or provide a large-print copy. Allow tally marks, counters, or colour coding. If timing individual learners may create anxiety, time table groups and compare methods rather than people. With no timer, compare completion order qualitatively and focus on discrepancies. In a 25–30 minute period, run one tally, discuss scale, and retain the exit statement.

## Period 2 — Compare speed, reliability, and accuracy (LO-1)

**Outcome check:** Learners distinguish measured speed, reliability, accuracy, reproducibility, and setup effort when comparing workflows.

### 1. Retrieval and vocabulary contrast — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Ask learners to retrieve one observed strength and one observed limitation of the manual tally. Then present two cases:

- a calculator that will not switch on;
- a spreadsheet that consistently counts the wrong range.

Ask: “Which concerns reliability, and which concerns accuracy?” Give **8 seconds**, pair-check, then hear explanations.

Suggested phrasing: “Reliability is dependable operation or availability. Accuracy asks whether a value matches its source or reality. A system can run reliably and produce inaccurate results.”

### 2. Digital workflow demonstration — 20% (8–9 minutes)

Enter the fictional codes into a spreadsheet or demonstrate a prepared version. Think aloud through setup, range selection, counting, and total verification.

Say: “Technology does not remove the need to check. I must confirm that all 20 records are present, the counting rule is appropriate, and the totals sum to 20.”

Before revealing the result ask: “Which is likely to take longer at this small scale: manual counting or entering and configuring a spreadsheet?” Give **7 seconds wait time**. Accept that setup may make manual work competitive for a small one-off task.

Low-resource equivalent: draw a table representing a spreadsheet and provide hypothetical setup and recount times. The comparison still depends on requirements and evidence.

### 3. Error-at-scale challenge — 20% (8–9 minutes)

Deliberately show or describe a formula that omits the final five rows. Ask learners first to predict whether the computer will warn the user automatically.

Likely response: some expect software to detect the problem. Reply: “The formula is syntactically valid. The machine follows the selected rule consistently; it does not know our intended range.”

Ask:

- “Is the system operating reliably?”
- “Is the result accurate?”
- “What control exposes the error?”

Use **5 seconds between questions**. Elicit source-record totals, independent recount, formula inspection, and reconciliation.

Misconception response—“computer means correct”: say, “Automation can make a good rule faster—and can make a bad rule wrong at greater speed and scale.” Ask learners for one prevention and one detection control.

### 4. Evidence comparison matrix — 30% (12–14 minutes)

Pairs compare manual and digital methods against speed, accuracy, reproducibility, setup effort, retrieval, and scale. For every judgement they must add a condition or observation.

Use prompts:

- “At what record volume?”
- “One-off or repeated task?”
- “What evidence supports ‘faster’?”
- “Which human check still remains?”

Assign one learner as recommender and one as sceptic; the sceptic challenges any absolute claim. Switch roles halfway.

Energy/pacing change: after quiet table work, conduct three rapid “always, sometimes, never” statements: “Manual is cheaper”; “digital is more accurate”; “automation removes people.” Require a reason for each “sometimes.”

### 5. Individual distinction check — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Learners answer Worksheet questions 1–3 or the compressed prompt: “Explain unreliability and inaccuracy using different examples, then explain how a wrong automated rule can scale error.”

Transition: “So far we compared two workflows. Next we examine the broader change from isolated electronic processing to information creation, storage, retrieval, and communication.”

**Differentiation and fallbacks:** Provide the matrix headings and sentence frames: “Method ___ is more suitable when ___ because ___; control ___ is still needed.” Learners with strong prior knowledge should add auditability and recovery. If devices fail, use a teacher-prepared paper trace of correct and omitted-row results. If time is short, preserve the error-at-scale challenge and individual check.

## Period 3 — How electronic technologies changed information work (LO-2)

**Outcome check:** Learners explain how electronic processing, storage, networking, and communication collectively support the creation, management, and dissemination of information.

### 1. Daily-life purpose and workflow reconstruction — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Write or display four actions in mixed order: `store records`, `calculate a summary`, `retrieve the summary`, `send it to another location`.

Assign pairs one daily-life purpose—**decision-making, policy formulation, forecasting, planning, surveying, or monitoring**. Ask them to name a fictional, non-identifying example and arrange the actions for that purpose, identifying which actions concern creation, management, or dissemination. Examples include planning bus capacity from aggregate counts, forecasting supply from past totals, surveying public facility availability, or monitoring a machine temperature; avoid personal profiles.

Ask: “How does the intended use change what information must be created and how quickly it must reach the user?” Allow **8–10 seconds**, because learners must connect purpose to workflow. Accept multiple action orders when justified.

Say: “We are studying capabilities and their convergence, not memorising invention dates.”

### 2. Before-and-after scenario — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Use a fictional clinic preparing a general vaccination-hours notice with **no patient data**. Contrast a paper-only workflow with an electronic and networked workflow.

Suggested narration: “One tool may create and store the notice; dissemination technologies such as broadcast media, telephony, mobile messaging, email, or Web publishing can carry it; different devices can retrieve it. Together, information and communication technologies reduce the distance and delay between creation and use.”

Ask, with **5–8 seconds wait time**:

- “Which capability creates the information product?”
- “Which manages versions or retrieval?”
- “Which disseminates it across locations?”
- “Which dissemination channel fits an urgent, wide public audience, and what access limitation might exclude someone?”
- “What could still go wrong faster at scale?”

Likely responses include quick editing, searchable storage, wide distribution, outdated copies, incorrect source facts, access failure, or sending to the wrong audience.

### 3. Capability timeline, not date recall — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Groups arrange capability cards:

1. isolated electronic calculation;
2. electronic storage and retrieval;
3. connected exchange between computers;
4. networked services and mobile access.

For each transition, learners state what new information task becomes easier and what new control becomes important. Examples: backup for storage, access control for shared systems, version control for dissemination, offline recovery for mobile use.

Transition: “Each new capability expands reach, but also expands dependence and the consequences of error.”

Misconception response—“technology replaced communication”: ask, “If information is created but cannot reach its user, has the complete task succeeded?” Draw attention to convergence rather than replacement.

### 4. Information journey mapping — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Groups map a fictional weather-warning journey using arrows: source observation → electronic processing → stored message → selected dissemination technology → network or broadcast transmission → user device → decision. They label where information is created, managed, and disseminated, and why the channel fits the audience and urgency.

Ask: “Where could quality fail?” and give **10 seconds**. Require at least one human/procedure issue and one technology issue.

Energy change: groups stand for a 45-second gallery scan or pass diagrams clockwise; each reviewer adds one control.

### 5. Exit explanation — 10% (4–5 minutes)

Prompt: “Electronic technologies enabled information work by ___, while communication technologies enabled ___; their convergence matters because ___.”

Transition: “Networked information work leads to a common confusion. Next we separate the Internet infrastructure from one service that runs over it—the Web.”

**Differentiation and fallbacks:** Provide capability cards with icons and key verbs. Accept a spoken explanation or labelled diagram. With no display, narrate the scenario and draw the journey on the board. Extension learners should identify a control at every stage. With limited time, combine the timeline and journey map but retain the exit explanation.

## Period 4 — Networks, Internet, World Wide Web, and domains (LO-3)

**Outcome check:** Learners describe the Internet as interconnected network infrastructure and protocols, the Web as a linked-resource service using it, and classify other Internet services separately.

### 1. Diagnostic sort — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Display: router forwarding a packet, opening a linked page, sending email, following a hyperlink, and transferring a file.

Ask learners to sort privately into `Internet`, `Web`, or `not sure`. Give **10 seconds**, then compare in pairs. Do not correct yet.

Say: “The Internet and Web are related, so confusion is understandable. Our goal is to describe the relationship precisely.”

### 2. Development and road-and-service model — 20% (8–9 minutes)

Draw a simple shared infrastructure carrying several labelled services: Web, email, and file transfer.

Suggested phrasing: “The Internet is the interconnected network infrastructure and protocol system that carries data. The World Wide Web is a service of linked resources accessed over that infrastructure. Email also uses the Internet, but an email is not a Web page.”

Build a three-step conceptual development sequence on the board: `stand-alone computers → connected local/wide networks → Internet-scale interconnection and services such as the Web`. Say: “This is a capability sequence, not a claim that every organisation changed at the same moment. The Web later made linked resources easier to publish and navigate over Internet infrastructure.” Avoid unsupported date memorisation.

Ask: “If the Web service were unavailable but email still worked, would the Internet necessarily be down?” Give **8 seconds wait time**.

Clarify that the road analogy is only a model: Internet protocols and networks are not literally roads, and services can interact.

### 3. Human network demonstration — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Assign learners or cards as device, router, Internet path, Web server/resource, email server, and user. Trace two journeys:

- user requests a linked page;
- user sends an email.

After each stage ask: “Is this infrastructure, a Web action, or another Internet service?” Allow **3–5 seconds** before selection.

Energy/pacing change: the physical model raises movement; follow it with one minute of silent diagram annotation to consolidate language.

Misconception response—“everything online is the Web”: ask where email belongs, then require the sentence “Email uses ___ but is not necessarily ___.”

### 4. Classification, domains, and safe interpretation — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Learners complete Worksheet questions 4–5. Require two parts for each classification: category and reason. Then display fictional examples such as `www.library.gov.lk`, `learn.example.edu`, and `offers-example.com` and identify the **domain name** as the human-readable name used to locate an Internet resource.

Suggested phrasing: “Parts such as `.lk`, `.gov`, or `.edu` can provide clues about naming and context, but a familiar-looking domain is not proof that content is accurate or safe. Inspect the complete address, source, and connection; do not enter credentials from a link merely because its name looks official.”

Ask: “Which is safer evidence of identity: one familiar word somewhere in an address, or checking the complete domain and source through a trusted route?” Give **8 seconds** and invite a learner to explain how a misleading name could imitate trust.

Probe with:

- “What is carrying data?”
- “Is a linked resource being accessed?”
- “Could this service use Internet infrastructure without being a Web activity?”

Likely correct responses: router forwarding is infrastructure; opening a linked page and following a hyperlink are Web activities; sending email is another Internet service. A domain helps locate a resource, but does not guarantee truth, authority, or safety.

### 5. Individual relationship check — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Prompt: “Explain the relationship without using the phrase ‘they are the same.’ Include one non-Web Internet service and explain what a domain name does.” Learners write first, then explain to a partner in 20 seconds.

Transition: “The Internet provides connectivity for many services. Next we evaluate what happens when processing and communication move with the worker.”

**Differentiation and fallbacks:** Provide the frame “The Internet is ___; the Web is ___ that uses ___; ___ is another service; a domain name ___.” Let learners manipulate labelled cards before writing. If no room for movement, move magnets or paper labels on the board. Use only fictional or reserved example domains; do not ask learners to visit unknown sites. Extension: ask whether a mobile app must be a Web activity and require a conditional answer. If time is short, keep the diagnostic sort, the complete-domain safety check, and individual relationship check.

## Period 5 — Evaluate mobile communication, computing, and cloud support (LO-4)

**Outcome check:** Learners identify a task-specific benefit, constraint, consequence, and workable control for mobile computing.

### 1. Field scenario hook — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Present this fictional scenario: “A survey team records counts of damaged public streetlights across several areas. Connectivity is intermittent. No resident names or personal details are needed.”

Ask: “What can workers do at the location that would be harder with a fixed desktop?” Give **8 seconds**, pair-share, then collect responses such as immediate entry, location-based work, photographs without people, and later synchronisation.

Privacy cue: “Collect only what the task requires. Do not photograph faces, homes, number plates, or identifying details.”

### 2. Define the capability — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Show “Mobile computing.”

Suggested phrasing: “Mobile communication carries voice or data while users move or work across locations. Mobile computing adds portable processing and storage, using devices such as tablets and laptops as well as phones. Cloud services can provide remote storage, applications, synchronisation, or shared processing through a network, but they increase dependence on connectivity, accounts, providers, and appropriate security.”

Ask: “Would an offline tablet still be a mobile computer?” Wait **7 seconds**. Guide learners to distinguish mobile processing from currently available communication.

Then ask: “Would saving to cloud storage mean the file is physically floating above us?” Use the likely laugh to reset energy, then clarify that “cloud” refers to remotely provided computing resources reached through networks, not a physical cloud and not automatically a backup unless versioning/recovery is configured.

Misconception response—“mobile computing means smartphones”: invite examples of rugged tablets, laptops, or handheld scanners and ask what processing and communication each supports.

### 3. Benefit–constraint–control triads — 30% (12–14 minutes)

Groups receive or choose constraints: intermittent connectivity, low battery, device loss, small screen/input, unauthorised access, or conflicting offline edits. For each they construct:

`task benefit → constraint and consequence → practical control`

Examples to guide only after initial thinking:

- intermittent connectivity → unsent records or unavailable cloud service → offline capture with queued synchronisation and a tested local copy;
- low battery → interrupted work → charging plan/power bank and saved drafts;
- device loss → exposure or loss → strong authentication, encryption, remote management, least-privilege access, and backup;
- small input surface → entry mistakes → suitable form design and review step.

Give **10–15 seconds wait time** when asking learners to explain why a control addresses the specific consequence.

### 4. Scenario stress test — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Change one condition at a time:

- “There is no signal for four hours.”
- “Two workers edit the same record.”
- “A device is lost.”
- “A message asks the worker to sign in to the cloud through an unfamiliar link.”
- “The task grows from 20 to 2,000 records.”

Groups revise their workflow. Assign a privacy reviewer who may stop any suggestion that collects unnecessary personal data.

Energy change: run this as brisk 90-second rounds, then slow down for a final written recommendation.

For the unfamiliar-link stress test, require workers to stop, inspect the full sender/address, use a trusted saved route to the service, and report the suspicious message rather than follow it. Do not demonstrate with real credentials.

Ask: “Which limitation can be controlled but not completely removed?” Accept nuanced responses; controls reduce risk rather than guarantee success.

### 5. Individual evaluation — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Learners answer Worksheet question 6 or write: “For this field task, mobile computing/communication/cloud support ___ because ___. A constraint is ___, which could ___. Use ___ as a control because ___.”

Transition: “A benefit list is not yet a recommendation. Next you will select a complete workflow from evidence and show where people, procedures, and technology remain necessary.”

**Differentiation and fallbacks:** Use pictorial constraint cards and sentence frames. Permit oral recording or paired rehearsal before independent writing. If devices are unavailable, evaluate the scenario on paper; no real mobile device, live account, cloud sign-in, or personal phone is required. For extension, require a synchronisation-conflict procedure, cloud recovery check, and data-minimisation rule. If time is short, use connectivity, device-loss, and suspicious-link stress tests and retain the individual evaluation.

## Period 6 — Recommend and defend a workflow (LO-1, LO-2, LO-3, LO-4)

**Outcome check:** Learners recommend a manual, digital, or hybrid workflow from requirements and evidence, include necessary controls, and accurately use Internet/Web concepts where relevant.

### 1. Four-outcome retrieval — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Learners divide a page into four boxes and retrieve without notes:

1. one reason bulk manual work becomes difficult;
2. one daily-life information purpose and one way electronic/dissemination technologies support it;
3. Internet versus Web in one sentence, plus the role of a domain name;
4. one mobile or cloud constraint paired with a security/safety control.

Give **75 seconds silent recall**, then one minute to improve in pairs. Scan responses to choose one point for brief correction; do not reteach the whole sequence.

### 2. Recommendation brief — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Present a fictional requirement: “A community ICT club processes twelve renewals each year, retrieves past records occasionally, and must send members a general meeting notice. Recommend a manual, digital, or hybrid workflow. Do not include real member details.”

Ask learners to underline the requirements: scale, accuracy, retrieval, dissemination, and cost.

Say: “Choose from requirements and evidence, not novelty. More than one recommendation can be defensible.”

### 3. Team design with accountable roles — 25% (10–11 minutes)

Groups create a workflow using:

`capture → check → store → retrieve → disseminate → recover`

Assign roles:

- requirements analyst checks task fit;
- workflow designer selects methods;
- risk reviewer challenges errors, failures, and privacy;
- evidence speaker prepares the defence.

Require the recommendation to state where people, procedures, and technology each remain necessary. If it uses a linked notice page, learners must identify that as a Web resource using Internet infrastructure; email remains another Internet service.

Circulate with questions:

- “Which requirement justifies this tool?”
- “At twelve records, is setup effort proportionate?”
- “What detects an incorrect entry?”
- “How will a record be recovered?”
- “Are you collecting anything the task does not require?”

### 4. Defence and challenge — 20% (8–9 minutes)

Each group gives a **45-second recommendation**. Another group asks one challenge from a different perspective: cost, scale, accuracy, retrieval, connectivity, or privacy.

Give speakers **10 seconds** to think before responding. Evaluate reasoning rather than confidence or speaking speed.

Misconception responses:

- “Digital is automatically best”: ask which measured requirement outweighs setup, cost, and controls.
- “Manual needs no security”: ask who can see, copy, lose, or alter the paper.
- “Automation removes people”: ask who defines rules, checks exceptions, authorises access, and responds to failure.

Energy/pacing change: keep presentations brisk, then move to silent individual writing for assessment validity.

### 5. Independent recommendation — 15% (6–7 minutes)

Learners answer Worksheet questions 7–8 or write a recommendation addressing method, scale, accuracy, retrieval, cost, dissemination, and one control. They must explain how information and communication technologies work together without introducing personal data.

### 6. Exit reflection — 10% (4–5 minutes)

Use the slide prompt: “Name one task where a manual method remains appropriate and justify it.” Require a task condition such as low volume, one-off use, low setup cost, or unavailable infrastructure.

Then ask: “What evidence would make you change your recommendation?” This prevents method loyalty and checks evaluative thinking.

**Differentiation and fallbacks:** Offer a workflow template and word bank, but retain the same decision demand. Learners may present a labelled diagram plus short justification. If group presentations are impractical, pairs exchange recommendations and write one challenge. Extension learners should specify a hybrid method and identify a failure-recovery step. With 25–30 minutes, shorten team defence but preserve individual recommendation and exit reflection.

## After-lesson evidence notes

Record only non-identifying evidence about class learning:

- **LO-1:** Do learners use observed mechanisms and scale conditions, rather than claiming manual work is always poor?
- **LO-2:** Can learners connect a daily-life purpose—decision, policy, forecast, planning, survey, or monitoring—to electronic processing, storage, retrieval, and appropriate dissemination?
- **LO-3:** Can learners explain network-to-Internet/Web development, distinguish infrastructure from the Web, name another Internet service, and state the role and safety limits of a domain name?
- **LO-4:** Can learners distinguish mobile communication, mobile computing, and cloud support, then pair task benefits and constraints with privacy, security, and recovery controls?

Use patterns in responses to plan retrieval. Store any learner-specific progress evidence only in the repository’s approved private cohort location, never in lesson content or generated public outputs.
